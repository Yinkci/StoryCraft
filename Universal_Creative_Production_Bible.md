# UNIVERSAL STORYTELLING & PRODUCTION BIBLE

**Version:** 2.1  
**Updated:** October 20, 2026  
**Purpose:** A reusable guide for creating narrated visual stories with different characters, settings, genres, and storylines.

> **Core approach:** Tell an immersive story in conversational second-person POV. The viewer experiences events as “you.” Narration naturally connects what the viewer observes, thinks, decides, and does. Build clear, meaningful visual beats that can be illustrated and edited into a fast-moving video.

---

## 1. Scope and project separation

This document contains **universal creative and production rules**, not a specific series, episode, protagonist, or world.

Create separate documents for each project:

- **Project / Series Brief:** genre, tone, premise, intended audience, setting, and any departures from this guide.
- **Character & Visual Reference Sheet:** protagonist and supporting character appearances, clothing, expressions, reference images, and style palette.
- **World Rules:** setting-specific logic, threats, abilities, technology, or fictional rules (if needed).
- **Episode Script:** numbered scenes and exact narration, with no complete episode stored in this Bible.
- **Storyboard / Asset Manifest:** scene-to-image mappings and approved file names.

Never automatically reuse a previous project's character, clothing, zombies, store layout, palette, or storyline. Use the current project's approved references.

## 2. Narrative perspective and voice — DEFAULT

1. **Second-person POV:** Address the audience as *you, your, you're*. The viewer is the character experiencing events. A project brief may explicitly override this.
2. **One continuous narrator:** The narrator paraphrases other characters' words instead of switching into voice-acted dialogue. Avoid quoted character dialogue unless a project explicitly requests it.
3. **Conversational, natural delivery:** Sound like someone guiding the viewer through an unfolding experience, not a detached recap or a list of actions.
4. **Connected thought process:** Where useful, move from **observation → interpretation → instinct/reasoning → action → consequence**. Do not force every step into every scene.
5. **Stay within the viewpoint:** Narrate only what the viewer can plausibly observe, infer, or know at that moment.
6. **Avoid repetition:** Do not restate an established location or fact at the beginning of the next scene unless something has changed.
7. **Match narration to the exact illustrated moment.**

**Too mechanical:**

> You notice a strange noise. You walk to the door.

**Preferred conversational flow (split into individual visual beats in the final script):**

> You hear something moving on the other side of the door.
>
> You can't tell what it is, but opening it immediately doesn't seem like a great idea.
>
> So you look for another way to check first.

Use this as a **voice example**, not dialogue to repeat.

### Viewer-centered reasoning and instinct — LOCKED

The narrator must make the viewer feel that **you are personally interpreting the danger and deciding what to do**. When a meaningful decision is made, express the reasoning as the viewer’s own thought, rather than a detached narrator’s verdict.

- Prefer direct viewer-centered phrases such as **“you know that…,” “you realize…,” “you figure…,” “you can tell…,”** and **“you decide…”** when they naturally fit.
- Explain the **comparison or consequence** behind an important choice: why staying inside is safer *than joining a panicking crowd*, or why an alternate route is preferable *to a blocked exit*.
- Keep the narration conversational, as if a friend were walking you through what you are experiencing, not reciting an incident report.
- Link the observation, the viewer's conclusion, and the resulting action across consecutive scenes. The sentences should feel like one unfolding thought.
- **Do not** mechanically insert “you know” in every scene. Use direct action lines during urgent moments, and reserve explicit reasoning for choices that matter.
- Never assert that an option is definitely safe without evidence. The viewer may believe one choice is *safer* given what they can observe.

**Too detached:**

> And when people start biting each other, staying inside seems like a good idea.

**Preferred viewer-centered voice:**

> And when people start biting each other, you know that staying inside is probably safer than joining the crowd outside.

**Another example:**

> You realize something isn't right. And when things start getting this dangerous, you know it's better to keep your distance. So instead of following everyone else, you look for another way out.

These are examples of **voice and reasoning**, not fixed plot details or mandatory wording. Apply the same principle to comedy, mystery, science fiction, adventure, and other genres.

### Natural transitions

Let adjacent scenes connect with words such as *but, so, instead, that's when,* and *until* when they fit. Vary sentence openings; avoid beginning every scene with “You notice,” “You see,” or “You realize.” Short, direct sentences are useful during action. Not every scene needs internal commentary.

## 3. Story structure and logic

- **Establish interest quickly.** Prefer an immediate disturbance, discovery, unusual situation, or urgent question. One brief time/place setup is fine when it leads directly into the hook.
- **Keep events chronological** unless a flashback is intentionally part of the specific story.
- **Maintain clear cause and effect.** Changes in character behavior, danger, or circumstances should have believable causes within the story's own rules.
- **Escalate with meaningful developments.** Avoid prolonged inactivity, repetitive reactions, or padding.
- **Give the viewer a coherent thought process.** The protagonist can make mistakes but should not act irrationally solely to force the plot.
- **Integrate practical reasoning when appropriate to the genre.** Show choices through the story rather than delivering a lecture.
- **End with a meaningful result:** a resolution, discovery, changed situation, decision, or cliffhanger. Not every episode needs a jump scare or unresolved threat.
- **Tone and humor are project-specific.** Dry humor may work in some projects, but should never be automatically inserted into every story.

## 4. Runtime and pacing — DEFAULT TARGET

- **Target maximum:** 90 seconds per episode, unless a project brief specifies another length.
- **Planning narration budget:** Approximately 175–205 spoken words for brisk delivery with some room for effects and transitions. This is not a mandatory word count.
- **Verify actual timing.** Count spoken words and time the narration. Do not claim a script fits 90 seconds merely because it has a particular number of scenes.
- **Visual rhythm:** Most still images should appear for roughly 1–3 seconds; vary based on audio and story emphasis.
- **Indicative scene count:** Roughly 35–45 visual beats may work for a 90-second video, but the count must follow the narration and meaningful changes, not a quota.
- **No default six-second static holds.** Longer holds require a clear dramatic reason.
- **Do not pad the script** to reach the time limit.
- If an approved full script exceeds the runtime, preserve the original and prepare a separately labeled shortened version rather than silently rewriting it.

## 5. Required script format — LOCKED

Write only the numbered scene label and exact narration, with a blank line between scenes. Each scene maps to **one distinct planned visual beat**.

**Every script must start at `SCENE 1`. Never use `SCENE 0`.** Number scenes sequentially without skips or duplicates. Any opening setup belongs in `SCENE 1`.

```text
SCENE 1
It's nearly midnight, and you're waiting for the last train.

SCENE 2
But just as the platform goes quiet, you notice a light moving inside the empty tunnel.

SCENE 3
At first, you assume another train is coming.

SCENE 4
Then you realize the light isn't moving along the tracks.
```

These are format examples, **not a canonical story**.

Split separate actions into separate scenes. Avoid cramming multiple changes of location, action, and reaction into a single scene. Keep neighboring scenes connected in narration even when the visuals cut rapidly.

## 6. Character and art direction — PROJECT-DEFINED

**Never assume a universal protagonist.** Before illustration, establish the current project's approved character and visual references.

The project reference sheet should specify:

- Main character's silhouette, anatomy style, face, proportions, clothing, accessories, and distinguishing features.
- Supporting characters' design family and how they differ visually.
- Background/environment art style, palette, lighting, texture, and atmosphere.
- Any genre-specific visual rules, such as how fictional creatures, injuries, or effects are depicted.

**Consistency rules:**

- Preserve the approved main character across every scene unless the story intentionally changes their appearance.
- Keep all characters within the **same approved design family**. For a stickman project, every character, including crowds and creatures, must use the approved stickman anatomy. For a different project, follow its own design specification.
- Preserve established locations, props, lighting, and character positions between consecutive shots.
- Do not add accessories or change outfits without narrative or reference support.
- Keep environments detailed enough to establish place while matching the selected illustrated style.
- Never silently transfer visual details from another project.

## 7. Illustration and asset production — DEFAULT LOCKED

1. Finalize the script and intended audio timing before making images.
2. Map **each numbered scene to one original image** unless the user explicitly asks for a different asset format.
3. Use the **current project's** approved reference images for characters and art style.
4. For every scene, specify the exact action, camera framing, environment, visible characters, and continuity details.
5. **Default final format: full-frame 16:9 widescreen.** If another aspect ratio is explicitly requested for a project, use that instead.
6. When asked for individual scene images, generate **independent full-frame compositions**, not a multi-panel comic sheet later cropped into squares.
7. **No blurred padding, fake widescreen extensions, stretched panels, borders, panel numbers, captions, speech balloons, or decorative text** unless explicitly requested.
8. Generate scenes in story order and inspect for character drift, incorrect action, or repeated compositions.
9. Do not regenerate the same scene in place of a requested next scene.
10. Deliver exactly the requested scene count. For example, Scenes 1–5 means **five images**.
11. Name files consistently: `Scene_01.png`, `Scene_02.png`, etc., matching the scene numbers.
12. Check actual image dimensions and contents before claiming that the set is complete.
13. If asked for a ZIP, package **existing approved original images** without restyling, cropping, stretching, or regenerating them.
14. If any images are missing or wrong, report the precise issue instead of presenting an incomplete or altered set as finished.

## 8. Content and platform suitability

- Follow the current project's audience and distribution-platform requirements.
- Prefer restrained, non-graphic depictions of injury or violence when broad social-platform distribution is a goal.
- Colors, effects, and content restrictions (for example, stylized pink blood) belong in the **project's visual reference sheet**, not as universal requirements.
- Changing a graphic element's color does not guarantee platform approval.
- Do not automatically include horror, zombies, violence, blood, or other genre-specific elements.

## 9. Quality checklist

### Story

- [ ] The script follows the project's POV and narration style.
- [ ] Scenes flow conversationally, with viewer-centered reasoning behind meaningful choices, without redundant setup or robotic action listing.
- [ ] The hook arrives quickly and developments have clear cause and effect.
- [ ] Choices, reactions, and consequences are believable within the story's rules.
- [ ] Every script begins at SCENE 1, with no SCENE 0 or numbering gaps.
- [ ] Each scene contains one drawable visual beat.
- [ ] The ending delivers a meaningful outcome or continuation point.
- [ ] The narrated runtime has been checked against the target.

### Visuals

- [ ] Current-project character and style references are followed.
- [ ] All characters share the approved anatomy/design family.
- [ ] Every image depicts the correct numbered scene.
- [ ] Image format matches the project specification (16:9 by default).
- [ ] No unrequested text, comic panels, square crops, or blurred padding.
- [ ] Visual continuity is maintained across scenes.

### Delivery

- [ ] Correct number of individually composed images.
- [ ] Files have the correct names and dimensions.
- [ ] Any archive contains the original approved images unchanged.
- [ ] Missing or failed assets are disclosed clearly.

## 10. Reusable instruction for future projects

> Follow the **Universal Storytelling & Production Bible** together with the **current project's brief, character sheet, visual references, and world rules**. Write immersive, naturally connected second-person narration unless the project specifies another POV. Let observations lead naturally into the viewer’s own thoughts (e.g., “you know that,” “you realize”), comparisons between options, decisions, actions, and consequences without over-explaining. Keep a strong hook, chronological clarity, meaningful escalation, and a satisfying ending or continuation. Start every script at SCENE 1 (never SCENE 0); use short numbered scenes, one visual beat per scene, and verify the requested runtime. For illustrations, use the project's approved art style and characters, preserve continuity, and deliver original full-frame 16:9 images by default, individually named and free of unrequested text or padding. Do not import characters, settings, plot details, or genre-specific restrictions from other projects.

---

**Precedence:** Explicit instructions in the current request and its project-specific reference documents override these defaults. This Bible is a reusable method, not a character sheet or an episode script.
